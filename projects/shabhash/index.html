The problem and discovery

A communication tool is also an adoption system.

The product was designed for children who were non-speaking, had delayed or unclear speech, or used single words. Caregivers, educators, clinicians, NGOs, and schools shaped whether it could be set up, trusted, and used.

Lived problem exposure

NGO and school visits exposed the everyday cost of not being able to express a need, choice, or feeling.

Stakeholder conversations

Doctors, psychiatrists, NGO workers, and school administrators helped pressure-test the need and usage context.

Early product validation

Open conversations and basic surveys separated essential communication support from avoidable training burden.

The child was the user. The surrounding adults shaped adoption, setup, and trust.

The product

Make the first useful communication loop simple.

The core interaction was intentionally direct: choose a picture or type, build a short phrase, then play it aloud. The product was meant to support everyday expression, not replace clinical care.

Product development

The MVP needed a development brief, not a feature wish list.

I translated the user journey into a tablet-first interaction model, recruited a small developer team, and planned an alpha release before broader distribution. The technology plan explored speech, cloud, and image services without making them prerequisites for the first useful version.

Planned product architecture

Translate one human need into a buildable system.

The development brief connected the child’s communication loop with caregiver setup, speech services, and a staged alpha plan.

What changed after conversations

The useful work was not defending the first idea. It was changing it.

Early feedback changed both the interface and the product model. These were direction-setting pivots, not proof of clinical efficacy or scaled adoption.

Language barriers were part of the problem.

Plan multilingual support for regional contexts.

Separate aided-language training felt like extra work.

Move guidance into the product experience.

Caregivers needed a faster emergency path.

Add an alert action with caregiver notification.

A phone could be too complex for some children.

Explore a simpler dedicated-device direction.

Building the venture

Product decisions became company-building decisions.

The venture plan connected discovery, scope, hiring, funding, distribution, and milestones. It explored NGO- and school-led reach, a freemium model, future hardware, and staged development.

What this experience built

My product instinct started before my product title.

Shabhash taught me to begin with the human problem, bring domain voices into the room early, and let evidence change the solution.

It also exposed the operating surface of a venture: form the team, protect the MVP from over-scoping, plan distribution, and make the case coherent enough to pitch.
